What is the Saga Pattern?

A Saga is a sequence of local transactions. Each step in the saga updates data within a single service's database.

If all steps succeed, the saga completes. If any step fails midway, the saga executes a series of compensating transactions to undo the changes made by the preceding steps.

The Two Core Approaches

There are two primary ways to implement and coordinate a Saga: Choreography and Orchestration.
1. Choreography (Event-Driven / Decoupled)

In Choreography, there is no central controller. Services listen to events emitted by other services and decide when to act.

    Happy Flow:

        Order Service saves an order (PENDING) and emits an OrderCreated event.

        Inventory Service listens to OrderCreated, reserves stock, and emits a StockReserved event.

        Payment Service listens to StockReserved, processes payment, and emits a PaymentProcessed event.

        Order Service listens to PaymentProcessed and updates the order status to APPROVED.

    Failure / Compensation Flow:

        If Payment Service fails, it emits a PaymentFailed event.

        Inventory Service listens to PaymentFailed and runs a compensating action: restores the stock.

        Order Service listens to PaymentFailed and updates status to CANCELLED.

    Best For: Simple workflows with 2 to 4 services where you want tight decoupling.

2. Orchestration (Central Controller)

In Orchestration, a dedicated Saga Orchestrator service (or a state machine component inside Order Service) controls the process. It explicitly tells each service what command to execute and handles response events.

    Flow:

        Order Service calls Saga Orchestrator.

        Orchestrator sends command Reserve Stock to Inventory Service.

        Inventory Service replies Stock Reserved.

        Orchestrator sends command Process Payment to Payment Service.

        Payment Service replies Payment Failed.

        Orchestrator sends compensation command Release Stock to Inventory Service.

        Orchestrator marks the order as CANCELLED.

    Best For: Complex workflows involving many steps, conditional branches, or long-running steps.
